Flight displays, explained

Reading a Head-Up Display

What a HUD is, how every symbol on it works, how to read one at a glance, and which camera of your simulator should carry it.

The short answers

  • How it works: a small display is placed at the focal distance of a lens. The lens makes the light travel in parallel lines, a sheet of angled glass (the combiner) reflects it to the pilot's eye, and the picture appears to float far out in front of the aircraft. The pilot sees the real world and the symbols together, with no refocusing.
  • How to read it: find the flight path vector (the little bird). It shows where you are really going. Compare it with the horizon and the fixed cross for attitude and angle of attack, then glance at the tapes for speed, height and heading.
  • Where to put it in your sim: on the forward-looking camera, the one fixed to the aircraft's nose. Not on the rear camera, and not as the main overlay on the gimbal. The gimbal gets its own sensor overlay. Details and a live demo are in section 3.

1. How a HUD works

A normal screen is a few tens of centimetres from your eyes. To read it, your eyes must focus close, then re-focus to the runway far away. That refocusing takes about a second and costs attention at the worst moment. A HUD removes it by making the symbols look as far away as the scenery.

Two small pieces of maths explain why the image looks far away and how big the HUD window is.

The lens equation
s′= s·fs−f

s is how far the display sits from the lens, f is the lens's focal length, and s′ is where the eye thinks the picture is. Put the display exactly at s = f and the bottom of the fraction becomes zero, so s′ → ∞. That is collimation: the picture appears infinitely far away.

Size of the HUD window
FOV=2·arctan (w2f)

w is the width of the lens opening. A wider lens or a shorter focal length gives a bigger window, but bigger optics are heavier and harder to fit in a cockpit. This is why real HUDs show only a window of roughly 20 to 30 degrees, not the whole canopy.

Three words you will see a lot

Collimated

Light rays made parallel, so the image seems very far away. This is the trick above.

Conformal

A symbol that sits on top of the real thing it describes. The horizon line lies on the real horizon. The flight path symbol lies on the real spot you are heading toward. A conformal symbol is only correct if the camera looks the way the aircraft's nose looks.

Combiner

The clear angled glass that adds the projected symbols to the real view. In a game it is simply the overlay on top of your 3D camera.

2. A live HUD you can poke

Click any part of the display, or any name in the list, to see what it is and how to read it. Move the sliders to fly it. The nose angle and the flight path are separate sliders on purpose: the gap between them is the angle of attack, and that gap is the thing a flight path vector exists to show.

The maths behind the readouts

Flight path angle
γ=arcsin( VvertV)

Climb or descent angle γ from the vertical speed Vvert and the speed V, both in the same units (feet per second). The bird sits γ degrees above or below the horizon.

Angle of attack
α≈θ−γ

Nose angle θ minus path angle γ. It is the vertical gap between the fixed cross and the bird. This version assumes wings roughly level and no wind effects; it is the picture, not a sensor reading.

Drift angle
β=χ−ψ

Track χ (where you travel over the ground) minus heading ψ (where the nose points). The bird moves sideways by β degrees. In a crosswind the nose points into the wind and the bird shows the real path.

Steady level turn
n=1cos⁡φ ω=g·tan⁡φV

Bank φ in a level turn raises the G load n (about 1.15 G at 30°, 2 G at 60°) and sets the turn rate ω, where g = 9.81 m/s² and V is in m/s.

How to read it, in order

  1. Where am I going? Look at the bird, not the nose. Above the horizon line you are climbing, below you are descending, left or right of the centre you are drifting sideways.
  2. Where is the nose? Find the fixed cross. The distance between the cross and the horizon is your pitch. Count rungs: each rung is 5 degrees on this display.
  3. How hard am I flying the wing? Look at the gap between cross and bird. A bigger gap below the cross means a higher angle of attack, closer to a stall.
  4. Bank. The ladder tilts and the pointer slides along the arc at the top.
  5. Numbers. Speed on the left, height on the right, heading on top. Tapes move opposite to the change: climbing makes the altitude numbers slide down.
Check yourself: choose the Final approach preset. The bird sits on the runway's aim point, with the cross about 8° above it. Now choose Crosswind. The nose points 5° off the runway, yet the bird still sits on it. That is the symbol doing its job: the path is lined up, even though the nose is not.

3. Where should the HUD go in your simulator?

The question is really: which camera is allowed to show conformal symbols? Conformal symbols only make sense when the camera looks along the aircraft's own forward direction, because the whole point is that they sit on the real horizon and the real flight path.

Camera viewFull flight HUD?What to show instead or as well
Forward / nose / cockpit cameraYesThe complete HUD: pitch ladder, horizon, bird, bank scale, tapes. This is the one place where every symbol is honest.
Gimbal (sensor) cameraOwn overlayA sensor overlay: centre reticle, gimbal azimuth and elevation, zoom or field of view, and a small strip with speed, altitude and heading. Real sensor displays work this way. The horizon and ladder are not drawn, because the camera is pointing somewhere else.
Rear / chase cameraNoNothing conformal. The bird would point behind the camera. If you want data here, use the same small strip as the gimbal view, or a corner attitude indicator.

Recommendation: make the HUD a module that is switched on only while the forward camera is active, and keep one shared data source (speed, altitude, attitude, path angle) that both the HUD and the small strip read from. Then no value is drawn twice on screen, and each view shows what is true for it.

Try it: why the bird cannot follow a turning camera

The bird is a direction in the world. The camera is a window pointed somewhere. Turn the camera away from the nose and watch the bird slide off the screen.

Where a world direction lands on screen
ypx= H2· tan⁡Δtan⁡(FOVv2)

Δ is the angle between the camera axis and the direction you want to mark (the path, or a ladder rung). H is the screen height in pixels and FOVv the camera's vertical field of view. If Δ reaches 90° or more, the direction is beside or behind the camera and the symbol must be hidden.

Building it in Unity, step by step

  1. Make one UI group called HUD and attach it to the forward camera only. Switch it on and off with the camera, so the rear and gimbal views never show it.
  2. Make the tapes, heading strip and readouts pure 2D. They need no projection at all, so they cost almost nothing per frame.
  3. For the bird, take a point far ahead along the velocity: the aircraft position plus the normalised velocity times about 1000 metres. Convert it with the camera's WorldToScreenPoint. If the returned depth is negative, hide the symbol.
  4. For the pitch ladder, use the same idea. Each rung is a world direction at a fixed elevation angle in front of the current heading. Project one point per rung. This keeps the ladder correct for any field of view, so you never have to tune pixel-per-degree by hand. Only rungs inside the window need to be shown.
  5. Rotate the whole ladder group by the negative of the roll angle, and keep the fixed cross at the screen centre of the forward camera.
  6. For the gimbal view, make a second small group: reticle, two angle readouts, the field of view, and the speed, altitude and heading strip.

All of this is about ten to twenty projections per frame, so it is cheap even on a modest machine.

Display layouts differ between aircraft (for example, some put the heading at the bottom). The numbers and symbols here follow the common arrangement of a classic fighter-style HUD, simplified for teaching.
